\section*{Discrete version}
Assume that $k$ targets have been identified at radial positions $\theta_i$, each with signal strength $s_i$. A consensus direction will be chosen from among these discrete targets using a neural model.

\subsection*{Background}
Consider a brain characterized by a recurrent neural network composed of $N$ spins. Each spin is associated with one of the $k$ targets (so there are $k$ groups of spins) and exists in one of two states: $\sigma_i=0$ or $\sigma_i=1$ - e.g., a neuron that is not firing or firing. The energy of the system is given by its Hamiltonian,
\[
H = -\frac{k}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}J_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j,
\]
where $J_{ij}$ is the interaction strength between spins $i$ and $j$. The sum over all $i\neq j$ assumes a fully-connected network of spins/neurons (all interact with all), and only pairs of ``on'' spins contribute to the energy. A positive $J_{ij}$ indicates an excitatory interaction between spin $i$ and spin $j$ which lowers energy (consensus lowers the energy of the system) while a negative $J_{ij}$ indicates an inhibitory interaction that raises the energy.

The decision to divide by the total number of neurons $N$ in the Hamiltonian normalizes the energy with respect to the size of the network and keeps the energy from blowing up as $N$ gets large. The inclusion of $k$ is never explained in the published papers, but it has the effect of making the energy landscape steeper as the number of targets increase. This means that you are more likely to get stuck in local minima - i.e., you need more temperature in order to explore the entire space. This could be biological - see Claire Hemingway's work on choice overload. In Grobonos's PRXLife paper, he also multiplies by a constant representing velocity. This would imply that the faster an organism is moving, the steeper the energy landscape and again, the less ability to explore the space. Also probably quite biological! 

The idea is that the neurons will seek to find a state which minimizes the energy of the system, and this state will represent a consensus velocity given by averaging all ``votes''/on-states:
\[
\vec{V} = \frac{v_0}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{u}_{\sigma_i}\sigma_i.
\]
Here, $\hat{u}_{\sigma_i}$ is the unit vector in the direction $\theta_i$ that is associated with $\sigma_i$, $v_0$ is a proportionality constant, and $\vec{V}$ is a consensus velocity.

Computationally, this minimum can be found using the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm, where a change in neural state $\sigma_i$ is dependent on the change of energy $\Delta H$ that accompanies it according to the Boltzmann weight
\[
P^{(i)}_{1\rightarrow0/0\rightarrow 1} = 
\begin{cases}
	\exp(-\Delta H/T) & \Delta H \geq 0\\
	1 & \Delta H < 0
\end{cases}
\]
where $P^{(i)}_{1\rightarrow0/0\rightarrow 1}$ is the probability that a spin switches its state and $\Delta H$ is the change in energy if the spin switches its state. I'm not sure how candidate spins are chosen each time. $T$ is the temperature parameter which can be interpreted as neural noise. As $T\rightarrow\infty$, all spins always turn on, and the decision will always be the average direction weighted by the number of spins associated with each target. As $T\rightarrow 0$, spins always turn on when the energy is lowered and never turn on otherwise. This will find local minima but won't explore the state space.

Another way of simulating this is to use Glauber dynamics. In this framework, a (uniform) random spin is chosen and the probability that the spin is flipped is given by the Fermi function,
\[
P^{(i)}_{1\rightarrow0/0\rightarrow 1} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\Delta H/T}}.
\]
This is quite similar to Metropolis-Hastings for low temperature ($T<2$), and particularly for $\Delta H > 0$. The difference is that $\Delta H = 0$ always has a switching probability of $0.5$ and there is a similar likelihood of failing to accept a lower energy state as there is failing to reject a higher one. The case where $T\rightarrow\infty$ is the same as in Metropolis-Hastings (all spins turn on and the decision is the average direction), but now $T\rightarrow 0$ has a similar result: turning on/off is reduced to a coin-flip in all cases, so the expected value result is again the average direction, but with only half the spins on. This means that in the derivation below, $T\rightarrow 0$ recovers an averaging model. We use Glauber dynamics below because it has the advantage of not being a piece-wise defined function: we don't have to know the sign of $\Delta H$ in order to specify the transition probabilities.

\subsection*{Derivation of model}

Now suppose we let the total number of neurons/spins $N$ get large. However, in a departure from Couzin's group, we will not assume that the size of each of the $k$ groups is the same. Instead, let's suppose that groups' relative sizes are given by the signal strength $s_i$ paired with the spins' associated direction $\theta_i$. The fraction of the total number of active neurons associated with $\theta_i$ is still given by
\[
n_i = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in G_i}\sigma_j
\]
where $G_i$ is the group of neurons associated with $\theta_i$. We will now make a second change from Couzin's group and define a \textit{coherence value} $\gamma\in\mathbb{C}$ given by averaging complex unit vectors according to the relative number of active neurons associated with them,
\begin{equation}
\gamma = \sum_{j=1}^{k}n_j e^{i\theta_j}.\label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
Here, $\gamma = Re^{i\Theta}$ with $0\leq R\leq 1$ representing the \textit{coherence strength} and $\Theta$ representing the \textit{consensus direction}.

Instead of simulating the process of each individual neuron turning on and off, we would like to put together a mean-field model. Assume we have a current coherence value, $\gamma$, corresponding to the state of the neural ring. This means that the mean effect of the ring amounts to a fraction $R$ of the $N$ neurons collectively being on and choosing the direction $\Theta$. 

We would like to know the change in energy associated with turning on or off one additional neuron. $RN$ neurons are turned on, so if we turn on one additional neuron associated with direction $\theta_j$, $\Delta H = -2kRJ_{\Theta,j}$ (the 2 is there because the new neuron interacts with $RN$ neurons twice, once on the left and once on the right). Similarly, if we turn one neuron off, the change in energy is approximately $\Delta H = 2kRJ_{\Theta,j}$. Here I construed $J_{\Theta,j}$ to be the interaction kernel between directions $\Theta$ and $\theta_j$.

So, Glauber dynamics give us approximate rates of switching equal to
\[
r^{(i)}_{1\rightarrow 0} = \frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)}\ \ \ \ \ \ r^{(i)}_{0\rightarrow 1} = \frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)}.
\]
Now we find the master equation in the limit as $N\rightarrow\infty$ as in the PNAS paper, and rearrange. But now, since the groups of neurons are of unequal size, the result is
\[
\frac{dn_j}{dt} = \frac{\frac{s_j}{s_{\text{total}}}-n_j}{\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)\right)} - \frac{n_j}{\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)\right)}
\]
\[
\frac{dn_j}{dt} = \frac{s_j}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)\right)} - n_j
\]
where $s_j/s_{\text{total}} = |G_j|/N$, $s_{\text{total}} = \sum s_j$.

Regarding the interaction term in the denominator: can work with a type of interaction kernel that is similar to the PNAS paper (see further notes in section below), where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $\theta_i$ and $\theta_j$.
\[
J_{ij} = \cos\left(\pi\left(\frac{|\theta_{ij}|}{\pi}\right)^\nu\right)
\]
$\nu=0.5$ was used in the PNAS paper and is similar to Mexican hat (see Fig. S1, Table S1, and Fig. 1 for comparison with experiments). Note that if $\nu=1$, $RJ_{\Theta,\theta_j}$ is the same as $\gamma\cdot e^{i\theta_j}$ treating the complex numbers as real valued vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Assuming neural updates are fast compared to the physical dynamics of the system, we can consider steady state solutions which satisfy
\begin{equation}
	n_j^* = \frac{s_j}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kR^*J_{\Theta^*,j}}{T}\right)\right)}.
	\label{eq:steadystate}
\end{equation}
Substituting into Eqn. \eqref{eq:gamma}, we see that steady state values for $\gamma$ satisfy
\begin{equation}
\gamma^* = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j e^{i\theta_j}}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kR^*J_{\Theta^*,j}}{T}\right)\right)}.
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
Equating real and imaginary parts then gives us a two equation system with unknowns $R^*$ and $\Theta^*$ that allow us to solve for these variables in steady-state configurations of the neural ring.

Now assume that the agent is facing a direction $\theta$ and updates this direction according to the torque model
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\theta}{dt} = KR^*\sin(\Theta^* - \theta).\label{eq:torqemodel}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is a physical constant related to turning speed. The idea here is that this a physical model of turning which describes the impetus for the animal to change its direction based on the direction it wants to go versus the direction it is currently facing and the coherence of that desired direction. 

Since by Eqns. \eqref{eq:gamma} and \eqref{eq:steadystate},
\[
R^*e^{i(\Theta^*-\theta)} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}n_j^* e^{i\theta_j-\theta} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j e^{i(\theta_j-\theta)}}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kR^*J_{\Theta^*,j}}{T}\right)\right)},
\]
we can apply Euler's formula and equate imaginary parts to get
\[
R^*\sin(\Theta^*-\theta) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j \sin(\theta_j-\theta)}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kR^*J_{\Theta^*,j}}{T}\right)\right)}
\]
In other words,
\[
\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{Ks_j\sin(\theta_j - \theta)}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kR^*J_{\Theta^*,j}}{T}\right)\right)}.
\]
This reveals that our torque model is a Kuramoto-type coupling with respect to target directions and with the coherence $R^*$ derived from the asymptotic dynamics of the neural ring. We can therefore view the Ising approach to consensus direction through neural energy minimization as a generalization of Kuramoto averaging over the targets. Regular Kuramoto averaging is the case when $T\rightarrow 0$, which corresponds to a gradient descent approach to the energy landscape - it will find local minima, but won't explore the state space. This suggests that the Ising approach may give more optimal results in general, because it allows for more exploration of the geometry before making a decision.

This model relies on steady-state solutions to the neural dynamics described in Eqn. \eqref{eq:model}. We want to consider only stable equilibia while keeping in mind that there may be multiple such solutions.

Again assuming that neural updates occur far faster than changes in the physical configuration of the system (including target angles and signal strengths), we can take the derivative of Eqn. \eqref{eq:gamma} to get
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{dn_j}{dt}e^{i\theta_j} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_je^{i\theta_j}}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ_{\Theta,j}}{T}\right)\right)} - \gamma\label{eq:dgamma}
\end{equation}
Note that this can be converted back to the PNAS paper equation by taking all $s_j=1/k$ and letting $\vec{V}=v_0(\text{Re}(\gamma),\text{Im}(\gamma))^T$. Numerically, to chose between potentially multiple stable equilibrium values of $\gamma$, we can solve this equation from an initial condition in order to determine which basin of attraction we are in.

To further analyze this model, we can determine stability of steady state solutions by considering what happens to a radially perpendicular perturbation nearby an equilibrium solution $\gamma^*$. This perpendicularity could be in the polar sense (a perturbation in $\Theta$), but $R$ also depends upon $\Theta$ so this gets complicated. Easier is to convert to Cartesian coordinates and consider a perturbation perpendicular to the vector given by the real and imaginary parts of $\gamma$.

Let $\vec{v} = (\text{Re}(\gamma),\text{Im}(\gamma))^T$, and let each $\hat{u}_j$ be a constant unit vector pointing in the direction $\theta_j$. Then $R=||\vec{v}||$, and Eqn. \ref{eq:dgamma} becomes
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j\hat{u}_j}{s_{\text{total}}\left(1+\exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)\right)} - \vec{v}\label{eq:dv}
\end{equation}
where $J(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)$ refers to the same interaction kernel as before, acting on the angle between $\vec{v}$ and $\hat{u}_j$. Now we will consider a perturbation perpendicular to the equilibrium solution $\vec{v}^*$, $\vec{v} = \vec{v}^* + \epsilon \hat{n}^*$ where $\hat{n}^*$ is orthogonal to $\vec{v}^*$. Eqn. \eqref{eq:dv} can be rewritten
\[
\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j\hat{u}_j}{s_{\text{total}}}f_j(\vec{v}) - \vec{v}
\]
where $f_j$ is the composition of the standard, scalar-valued logistic function $l(x)$ and a scaling of the interaction kernel $J$ with $\theta_j$,
\[
f_j(\vec{v}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\frac{2kRJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)} = l\left(\frac{2kRJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)
\]
This implies the vectorized equations in Eqn. \eqref{eq:dv} are similar,
\[
\frac{dv_m}{dt} = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{s_j\hat{u}_{j,m}}{s_{\text{total}}}f_j(\vec{v}) - v_m.
\]
Then expanding the right-hand side of Eqn. \eqref{eq:dv} around $\vec{v}^*$ with a perturbation normal to $\vec{v}^*$, we get
\[
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}\hat{n}^* = \left(\frac{1}{s_{\text{total}}}\left(\begin{array}{c}
	\sum s_j\hat{u}_{j,1}\nabla f_j(\vec{v}^*)	\\
	\sum s_j\hat{u}_{j,2}\nabla f_j(\vec{v}^*)
\end{array}\right) - \left(\begin{array}{cc}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{array}\right)\right)\epsilon\hat{n}^* + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)
\]
where $\nabla f_j(\vec{v})$ is here construed to be a row vector. Passing in $\hat{n}^*$, this can be simplified to
\[
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt}\hat{n}^* = \left(\frac{1}{s_{\text{total}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_j\hat{u}_j(\nabla f_j(\vec{v}^*)\cdot\hat{n}^*)
- \hat{n}^*\right)\epsilon + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2).
\]
Applying the chain rule, we see that
\[
\nabla f_j(\vec{v}) = l'\left(\frac{2kRJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)\frac{2k\nabla RJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T} = \frac{k}{2T}\text{sech}^2\left(\frac{kRJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)\nabla RJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j).
\]
So then, taking the dot product with $\hat{n}^*$ on both sides, we arrive at
\[
\frac{d\epsilon}{dt} = \left(\frac{k}{2Ts_{\text{total}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_j\ \text{sech}^2\left(\frac{kR^*J(\vec{v}^*,\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)(\hat{u}_j\cdot\hat{n}^*)(\nabla R^*J(\vec{v}^*,\hat{u}_j)\cdot\hat{n}^*)
- 1\right)\epsilon + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2).
\]
Setting
\[
A = \frac{k}{2Ts_{\text{total}}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}s_j\ \text{sech}^2\left(\frac{kR^*J(\vec{v}^*,\hat{u}_j)}{T}\right)(\hat{u}_j\cdot\hat{n}^*)(\nabla R^*J(\vec{v}^*,\hat{u}_j)\cdot\hat{n}^*),
\]
we conclude that the equilibrium $\vec{v}^*$ is locally asymptotically stable if $A<1$ and unstable if $A>1$.

If you replace $RJ(\vec{v},\hat{u}_j)$ with the dot product $(\vec{v}\cdot\hat{u}_j)$ (corresponding to the $\nu=1$ case), then the inner product of the gradient and $\hat{n}^*$ gives you $(\hat{u}_j\cdot\hat{n}^*)$, resulting in that term squared in the above formula and allowing you to recover the expression in the PNAS paper if you set all $s_j=1$ and $s_{\text{total}}=k$.

If $\nu\neq 1$, the result is ugly:
\[
\nabla RJ(\vec{v}^*,\hat{u}_j)\cdot\hat{n}^* = \nu\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \pi\left(\frac{\Theta^*-\theta_j}{\pi}\right)^\nu\right)\left(\frac{\Theta^*-\theta_j}{\pi}\right)^{\nu-1}.
\]

\subsection*{Note about signal strengths}
This discrete version assumes that radial target locations are identified by a separate perception process. If signal strength does not vary with distance, then the underlying assumption is that all targets are delta functions. Avoiding this, the best choice for signal strength is probably some measure of the perceived angular width of the target. The problem of not being able to tell two overlapping locusts from one close locust is avoided by the prior target identification, and size scales with distance in the proper manner (closer yields larger numbers while staying bounded) and is also biological in the sense that visual size of the object IS the actual signal reaching the eye.

\subsection*{Note about blind spots and smooth cutoff functions}

If the target is located directly behind the focal locust, the torque model has a discontinuity because, essentially, the locust cannot decide whether to turn to the left or the right in order to turn toward the target. Adding a perception blindspot helps, but there are decisions to be made when the targets have non-zero volume.
\begin{itemize}
	\item You can decide that if the target center (a zero-volume point) is in the blindspot, the target is completely invisible. But that results in weird cases where a close or massive target is invisible even when a significant portion of its volume is in the visual range. And then it suddenly appears in full when the center moves a little bit. This seems badly discontinuous.
	\item You can instead mask all visual signals within a blindspot, perhaps moving the assumed center of the target to the edge of the visual range  (this is what I have done). This does not solve the problem of a target directly behind entirely, because if the target is big/close enough to appear on both sides of the blindspot, you either have a discontinuity still (if you are not moving the assumed center of the target to the edge of the visual range) or you have a decision to make as to which side of the blindspot you are moving the target to. I view this last option as the least offensive because it should be both rare (a target has to be perfectly behind the focal locust to cause a decision issue) and computationally stable in terms of avoiding any mathematical discontinuities.
\end{itemize}

The interaction kernel for the neural ring should not have a blindspot - just because two angles are on opposite sides of the neural ring does not mean they are in a blindspot.

Just for the sake of writing this down, if you want a smooth cutoff function for the blindspot, it can be done like this. Let $\eta(t)$ be the standard bump function defined by:
\[
\eta(t) = 
\begin{cases}
	\exp\left( -\frac{1}{t} \right) & \text{if } t > 0, \\
	0 & \text{if } t \leq 0.
\end{cases}
\]
A smooth transition from 0 to 1 can be defined by:
\[
s(t) = \frac{\eta(t)}{\eta(t) + \eta(1 - t)}
\]
Then the smooth cutoff function $\psi(x)$ that equals 1 on $[a_1, b_1]$ and 0 outside $[a_2, b_2]$ (with $a_2 < a_1 < b_1 < b_2$) is given by:
\[
\psi(x) = s\left( \frac{x - a_2}{a_1 - a_2} \right) \cdot
s\left( \frac{b_2 - x}{b_2 - b_1} \right)
\]
Multiply this by any function you want to give it support only on a defined interval.


\section*{Continuous version}
An issue here is that since there are angles that don't have targets, you need this $\eta$ term in Gorbonos [41] to inhibit angles which don't have signal. Given that we are supposed to take $\eta N >> J$ and $N\rightarrow\infty$, maybe it isn't important, but it's hard to say.

I also think it's a mistake to focus overly on continuous incoming visual signal at the cost of identifying discrete objects in the visual field. Identification of visible objects and their definitive location in space is an important mental task that we can assume is accomplished by the brain of the creature we are considering. Otherwise, you are going to start asking yourself questions like ``can a locust tell the difference between two locusts that are overlapping each other in the visual field and one gigantic or really close locust?''
